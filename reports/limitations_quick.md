# Limitations and threats to validity (QUICK smoke run: tiny data, not results)

Generated by `scripts/run_critical_eval.py` (`src/critical_eval_writeup.py`) from the Step 0 data checks (`reports/data_check_quick.json`, `data/splits/splits_info_quick.json`), `config.py` and the Step 4 tables; no number is typed by hand. The findings these limits apply to are in [`critical_eval.md`](critical_eval.md).

## Data and features

* **Static features only.** Every model sees the 2,568 thrember features in 12 groups (general, histogram, byteentropy, strings, header, section, imports, exports, datadirectories, richheader, authenticode, pefilewarnings), computed from the file's bytes and PE structure without running it. Behaviour that only shows at run time (unpacking, network traffic, injected code) is invisible, and so is anything outside a Win64 PE file.
* **Packed and obfuscated files.** Packer tags in EMBER2024 exist only on malicious files, so the false-positive rate on packed *benign* software cannot be measured here; the model may simply have learned 'packed means malicious'. The feature-based proxy (whole-file entropy > 7) shows the cost: benign FPR on test_future is 1.08% for high-entropy files against 0.55% for the rest (`bias_slices_quick.csv`, 1%-FPR train threshold).
* **Dataset age and recency.** Training files are from 2023-09-24 to 2024-09-21, test_future files from 2024-09-22 to 2024-12-14. The model has never seen anything newer, and the drift measured over 12 weeks says little about drift over the years between the data and any use of this model today.
* **Drift horizon.** All temporal results cover 12 future weeks. The weekly AUC slope (+0.00055 per week, `drift_quick.csv`) comes with week-level CIs that overlap between the first (week 52) and the last (week 63) future week; a longer horizon could show more decay.
* **50/50 balance vs real prevalence.** The splits were built with 49.98% / 50.10% / 49.95% malware in train / test_random / test_future. Real traffic is mostly benign; at 1% prevalence the tuned model's precision on test_future would be 59.6% (`precision_both_sets_quick.csv`). The real prevalence of the place of deployment is unknown, so 1%, 5% and 10% are scenarios, not measurements.
* **Subsampling.** After deduplication the official data has 520,000 training-period and 119,993 test-period files. We used 8,000 for train and 2,000 for test_random (from the training weeks) and 2,000 for test_future, stratified by label x week; Step 2 tuned on a 5,000-row subset with 3 folds. Larger samples would narrow the CIs, and could change close rankings between tuners and tools.
* **Label noise from AV-derived labels.** A label is a verdict of antivirus engines: in our cache no benign file has more than 2 detections and no malicious file fewer than 5 (149 benign training-period files have at least one), so files in the ambiguous middle are not in the data at all, which makes the task easier than real triage. Labels can also be wrong in ways the engines share, and they change as engines catch up: the challenge set consists of files the engines first missed.
* **Near-duplicates.** Deduplication removes exact copies only (same sha256); variants of one malware family remain on both sides of the random split, which is part of why test_random is easy (per-family slices in `bias_slices_quick.csv`).
* **The duplicate rows in the raw data.** Each weekly file lists every sample twice (520,000 and 120,000 in-file duplicates in the training and test periods, plus 7 across test files). We deduplicate globally by sha256 before any split; without that, a random split would put copies of the same file into train and test and inflate test_random further. Anyone comparing with other EMBER2024 results should check whether they did the same.

## Threats from the evaluation itself

* **Adversarial evasion was not tested.** Static features can be changed without changing behaviour (appended bytes, added imports or sections, a valid signature). The models lean on signature columns (`explainability_quick.md`); detection of signed malware on test_future is 41.86% at the train 1%-FPR threshold, against 89.44% for unsigned malware, and the challenge set is detected at 19.4%. An attacker who knows this has an easy target.
* **One seed (42), one split per scheme.** The CIs cover test-set sampling only, not training randomness or another draw of the splits; time-budgeted AutoML searches are not repeatable, so deltas of about 1e-4 AUC between AutoML runs may be within run-to-run variation, which was not measured.
* **Many slices, no multiple-comparison correction.** Slice CIs are per slice; with dozens of slices some differences will look significant by chance. A test_random week has only about 385 benign files, so a single week's FPR there is very noisy.
* **Thresholds and calibrators come from smaller models.** They are fitted on train out-of-fold scores of fold models trained on two thirds of train, while the deployed model is trained on all of it (stated, not corrected; `threshold_quick.csv` shows the effect at high cost ratios, where the test_future FPR is above the out-of-fold FPR).
* **The forward CV confounds recency with training-set size:** later folds are both closer in time and trained on more weeks. A size-matched control exists only for drift (`drift_quick.csv`).
* **The AutoGluon leakage predictors were deleted** after scoring (about 2 GB each, little disk on the VM); their leaderboards, importances and test scores are saved, but those rows can be re-scored only by re-running them.
* **The challenge set is small and malicious only:** 814 Win64 files, so it measures misses, not false alarms.

## Compute, hardware and budgets

* **CPU only, and two machines.** Steps 2-3 ran on a 16-core Intel Core Ultra 9 285H laptop (63 GB RAM, Windows) with 16 threads; Step 4 on the machine of this quick run (Intel64 Family 6 Model 197 Stepping 2, GenuineIntel, 16 logical CPUs, 63.4 GiB RAM, Windows 11). Every comparison inside a step ran on one machine with the same settings, but times are not comparable across steps, and an AutoML tool searches fewer configurations per second on the VM.
* **Memory forced runner changes on the VM.** The runner settings were the same for every leakage variant: a lean FLAML preprocessing and a smaller H2O heap without XGBoost. AutoGluon itself skipped memory-heavy models (only in the detection_ratio run; in the clean and date runs its first LightGBM used the whole budget), so on the VM it was in effect a single LightGBM model (per-run counts in `leakage.md`).
* **The 30 s leakage budget** is a stated deviation from the 60 s of Step 3, to keep nine AutoML runs within a few hours. The clean reruns used the same budget and hardware, so each with/without-leak pair is equal-budget (not equal-compute: see below), but the clean scores are not the Step 3 scores (H2O: test_future AUC 0.9833 at 30 s on the VM vs 0.9891 at 60 s on the laptop). Budgets are not hard limits: the AutoML runs ended +5 s to +45 s from their budget (wall minus budget, `leakage_quick.csv`): the budget caps the search, and FLAML's final refit and H2O's data import, ensemble and scoring come on top, so the effective compute of one tool's variants differed by minutes (`leakage.md`, *Run times*).
* **Default AutoML settings.** We tested what a user gets by default (random-split validation, default presets); other settings, such as time-ordered validation, could behave differently.
