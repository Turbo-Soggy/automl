# Critical evaluation: how far do random-split scores overstate real-world performance, and does AutoML protect against leakage and drift?

Generated by `scripts/run_critical_eval.py` (`src/critical_eval_writeup.py`) from `reports/tables/random_vs_future.csv`, `reports/tables/precision_both_sets.csv`, `reports/tables/challenge.csv`, `reports/tables/leakage.csv`, `reports/tables/gap_vs_tuned.csv`, `reports/tables/automl_self_estimate.csv`, `reports/tables/cv_comparison.csv`, `reports/tables/drift.csv`, `reports/tables/gap_adversarial.csv`, `reports/tables/class_weight.csv`, `reports/tables/calibration.csv` and `eval_bias_facts.json`. Nothing here is typed by hand; the detail behind each section is in [`leakage.md`](leakage.md), [`temporal_bias.md`](temporal_bias.md) and [`explainability.md`](explainability.md), and the threats to validity are in [`limitations.md`](limitations.md).

**Machine.** Steps 2-3 (the tuning searches and the 1200 s AutoML runs, and the saved models scored here) ran on a 16-core Intel Core Ultra 9 285H laptop (63 GB RAM, Windows) with 16 threads. Step 4 ran on a cloud VM: Intel(R) Xeon(R) Processor @ 2.10GHz, 4 logical CPUs, 15.7 GiB RAM, Linux 6.18.44-fc-v185; the leakage runs used 4 threads (the part B and C fits 2, because those two jobs shared the machine). Every with/without comparison inside Step 4 was run on that machine with the same settings. No wall-clock time is compared across the two machines, and the AutoML scores at the Step 4 budget are not the Step 3 scores (fewer cores search fewer configurations in the same time). The challenge-set scores (sections 1, 2 and the verdict) were computed on a 16-CPU Windows machine (the laptop), which holds every saved Step 2-3 model; scoring fits nothing, so the hardware does not change those numbers.

**Test-set discipline.** No model, configuration, early-stopping point or threshold was chosen on test_random or test_future. The thresholds used below are 0.5, the 1%-FPR threshold set on TRAIN out-of-fold scores (`train_oof_1pct_fpr`) and the cost-sensitive deployment threshold in `models/threshold.json` (also set on TRAIN out-of-fold scores). CIs are 95%: bootstrap (1000 resamples; seed 42 for test_random, seed 43 for test_future) or Wilson for rates.

## The answer in short

1. **Ranking metrics barely move for the tree models.** From test_random to test_future the tuned LightGBM goes from AUC 0.9991 to 0.9981 (gap 0.0010 [0.0007, 0.0014]) and from TPR@1%FPR 98.7% to 97.6% (gap +1.07 pp [+0.67 pp, +1.46 pp]). The AUC gaps of all 7 tree models lie between 0.0010 and 0.0014; only LogReg, which cannot cap out-of-range feature values, loses much more (0.0632 AUC). By AUC alone, the random split overstates the tuned model's real-world performance only slightly.
2. **The overstatement is in the operating point and the base rate, which AUC hides.** At the threshold fixed on train (t = 0.5666) the false-positive rate rises from 0.95% on test_random to 1.23% on test_future (+29% relative). On the 50/50 test sets precision is 99.0% / 98.8%; at a realistic 1% malware prevalence it is 51.2% [47.6%, 54.7%] on test_random and 44.7% [41.6%, 47.8%] on test_future, i.e. 121 false alarms per 10,000 files scanned (random split: 94). Reading the 50/50 test_random as 1%-prevalence traffic changes precision by -47.87 pp; moving from test_random to test_future at that prevalence changes it by -6.50 pp: the 50/50 test mix is the larger source of optimism.
3. **The hard tail is invisible to both test sets.** On the 814 Win64 challenge-set files (malware that antivirus engines missed when first seen) the tuned model detects 53.7% [50.3%, 57.1%] at the same threshold, against 97.9% of ordinary test_future malware.
4. **No AutoML tool protects against the label leak.** With `detection_ratio` added, 4 of 4 models (tuned LightGBM and the three AutoML tools) ranked it first, by their own importance or by SHAP, and scored test_future AUC 0.9999 to 1.0000; 0 log lines named the column and 0 tools dropped it. The future split does not expose this leak either: a post-hoc label proxy is just as available (and perfect) in later weeks.
5. **AutoML does not protect against drift.** None of the 3 AutoML tools has a smaller AUC gap than the tuned LightGBM; a larger one: H2O AutoML (+0.0004 [+0.0002, +0.0007]) (paired bootstrap CI of the gap difference), `reports/tables/gap_vs_tuned.csv`. Every tool validated on a random split of `train` (the same weeks as its training rows): its own validation AUC was 0.9952 to 0.9994 on the clean runs, against test_future 0.9922 to 0.9980, and 1.0000 with the leak: a near-perfect score that no tool questioned.

* The two CV schemes bracket test_future AUC: stratified CV (every fold mixes all 52 weeks, like test_random) is optimistic, the forward CV (validate on later weeks only) pessimistic, partly because its folds fit on fewer rows (`temporal_bias.md`, section 2).

## 3. Why the random-vs-future gap is small for trees (and not a bug)

* **The inputs do drift.** A classifier separates train files from future files with AUC 0.9383 (control, same weeks: 0.4973); the three time-stamp columns carry 53.8% of its gain (`reports/tables/gap_adversarial.csv`, `reports/tables/gap_adversarial_top_features.csv`). Trees cannot extrapolate: a value beyond the last split lands in the same leaf as the newest training values, which limits what drift in such columns can do to the detector.
* **Removing the time proxies inside X does not shrink the gap.** Without the COFF timestamp and the two signing-time columns the tuned model's AUC gap goes from +0.00101 to +0.00107 (`reports/tables/leakage.csv`, `ablate_time_proxies`), so they are not a hidden time leak that flatters test_random.
* **No label shift.** The malicious share is 50.00% / 50.01% / 50.01% in train / test_random / test_future (`reports/tables/class_balance.csv`), by construction, so the score cannot move through the class mix.
* **Short horizon, slow decay.** Over the 12 future weeks the weekly AUC slope is -0.00011 per week (`reports/tables/drift.csv`); a model retrained on only the last 12 training weeks scores 0.9973 on test_future, against 0.9981 for the model trained on all 52: at this horizon, more data beats recency. At equal size, recent weeks may be worth slightly more than random weeks 0-51: 0.9973 vs 0.9969 for a size-matched random sample (higher in 10 of 12 future weeks; suggestive only, with no paired CI on this difference and one seed).
* **Weekly stratification** makes test_random an interpolation test: every test_random file has training files from its own week, which is the main reason it is the optimistic one.

*Relied on it* = kept the column and ranked it first by its own importance or by SHAP. FLAML's own importance (`feature_importances_` of its LightGBM learner) is the split count, which favours continuous high-cardinality columns such as a date, so for FLAML only its SHAP rank counts.

* **`detection_ratio` is a label leak, and every tool took it.** It is the share of antivirus engines that flagged the file, i.e. the thing the label was made from. A column with AUC 1.0000 against the label (`reports/data_check.json`) is exactly what a search for the best score is built to find, so no tool can be expected to reject it from the data alone; only a human question about provenance ('is this known when the file first arrives?') catches it.
* **`first_submission_date` is mostly harmless here, but for a fragile reason.** It moved test_future AUC by at most 0.0009. For 4 of 4 models that move excludes 0, but only under test-set resampling: the CIs do not cover the run-to-run variation of a time-budgeted AutoML search. H2O's move (+0.0009) is smaller than the gap between H2O's two clean searches (test_future AUC 0.9970 in Step 3 vs 0.9922 in the Step 4 clean run), but those two runs differ in budget and machine, so this is only indirect evidence of search noise: the true run-to-run variation was not measured (one seed per run). So no effect of the date column on the AutoML tools is shown. Contrary to the plan's expectation (a time leak that helps under the random split and fails on test_future), the date did not inflate test_random AUC except for H2O AutoML, possibly within the same (unmeasured) search noise, so this run does not demonstrate a time leak under the random split. Every future date lies after the last training date, so for a tree the column is a constant on test_future; with a 50/50 class mix in every week it carries almost no label information. In data where the malware share changes over time, the same column would let a model learn the calendar.
* **The tools' own validation did not raise the alarm either:** with `detection_ratio` their internal validation AUC was 1.0000 (`reports/tables/automl_self_estimate.csv`). A near-perfect AUC on malware detection should itself be treated as a bug report.

## 5b. Bias slices, explanations and cross-tool agreement

Sources: `reports/tables/bias_slices.csv` (tuned LightGBM, train 1%-FPR threshold), `reports/tables/shap_family_importance.csv`, `reports/tables/cross_tool_agreement.csv`, `reports/tables/cross_tool_top20.csv`; details in `temporal_bias.md` section 4a and `explainability.md`.

* **Weakest slices on test_future** (slices with at least 100 files of the class): the highest benign FPR is `file_size_quartile` = Q4, 2.86% [2.14%, 3.81%] of 1,540 benign files; the lowest detection rate is `signed` = signed, 83.7% [81.3%, 85.9%] of 970 malicious files.
* **What the tuned model uses** (test_future): by the summed per-column mean |SHAP| share, which favours families with many columns, strings (17.5%), imports (15.1%), histogram (12.8%); by the grouped measure (mean |sum of the family's SHAP|), datadirectories (2.00, 34 columns), authenticode (1.77, 8 columns), strings (1.69, 177 columns). The ranking depends on the measure; `explainability.md` explains both.
* **Do the tools agree?** By the rank correlation of feature-group importance, the most similar pair is tuned LightGBM / FLAML (Spearman 0.99, 4 of their top 5 features shared) and the least similar tuned LightGBM / H2O AutoML (0.32). Features in the top 5 of every tool: `authenticode: latest_signing_time`, `header: coff.char.DLL`. The measures differ by tool (SHAP, permutation importance, H2O varimp), so these are rough comparisons.
* **What this means for AutoML:** the AutoML winners are tree ensembles on the same features and, where the measures are comparable, they rank the same signals high. So they are likely to share the same blind spots, such as the weaker detection of signed malware: AutoML changes how a model is searched for, not what it can see.

## 6. What protects, and what does not

* **A provenance review of every column, before training** (human): the only defence against `detection_ratio` in this project. It is cheap and no tool did it.
* **A temporal hold-out** (train on earlier weeks, test on later ones): it measures drift and makes a date column useless, but it cannot catch a post-hoc label proxy (section 4).
* **Thresholds and costs re-derived for the real base rate.** The 10:1 cost threshold in `models/threshold.json` (t = 0.0069), chosen on 50/50 train data, has FPR 7.43% on test_future and precision 11.9% at 1% prevalence (`reports/tables/prevalence.csv`): fine on a balanced test set, costly in real traffic.
* **Class weights did not help:** 0 class-weighted refit(s) improved test_future AUC beyond the CI (`reports/tables/class_weight.csv`). With 50/50 classes there is no imbalance to correct, and reweighting only shifts scores, which a threshold does as well.
* **Calibration fitted on train out-of-fold scores carries over to later weeks:** isotonic calibration changes the test_future Brier score by -0.0003 [-0.0006, -0.0001] (`reports/tables/calibration.csv`). It makes the probabilities more trustworthy, not the ranking.
* **Retraining on recent weeks only did not help at this horizon:** 0.9973 vs 0.9981 test_future AUC (`reports/tables/drift.csv`). The real remedy, retraining with labelled files from the deployment period, cannot be tested without using test_future for training.
* **A stress set of hard files** (the challenge set) is what showed the largest real-world weakness; neither random nor temporal splits of ordinary data show it.

## 7. Verdict

For a tree model on EMBER2024 Win64, a random split overstates the ranking quality only a little (AUC 0.9991 vs 0.9981 on later weeks). The bigger overstatement comes from reading a 50/50 test set as if it were real traffic: at 1% prevalence only 44.7%, fewer than half, of the tuned model's alarms on test_future are real, and it misses 46% of the malware that antivirus engines missed at first. The AutoML tools' Step 3 models reach test_future AUC 0.9970 to 0.9980 (tuned LightGBM 0.9981), and they lose no less to drift: none of the 3 AutoML tools has a smaller AUC gap than the tuned LightGBM; a larger one: H2O AutoML (+0.0004 [+0.0002, +0.0007]) (paired bootstrap CI of the gap difference). They do not protect against leakage: given a post-hoc label column they all use it, report a near-perfect validation score, and say nothing. The protections that worked here were human ones: asking where each column comes from, testing on later data, and checking the operating point at a realistic base rate.
